> [!Background]
> Long before bedtime stories softened him, before laughter polished him into something bright and marketable, there was another.
> Not Tinker Bell.
> Fink Errsell.
> Among the oldest fairy circles, Fink is remembered (when he’s remembered at all) as the first to bind craft to instinct—the original “tinker,” though he never chose the title. Where other fair folk coaxed magic through song, emotion, or ritual, Fink forced it into shape. He hammered it, trapped it, bent it to purpose. Nails from lightning. Blades from broken promises. Wings patched with stolen shimmerdust.
> The elders called it unnatural.
> The other fairies called him worse.
> “Fairy” itself became a slur in his ears—soft, delicate, frivolous. Everything he wasn’t.
> 
> Fink’s exile might have been quiet, another forgotten misfit drifting between hedgerows and hollow trees… if not for the boy.
> Peter.
> He wasn’t yet the storybook figure. He was feral, reckless, half-lost between worlds. And Fink saw something familiar in him: a refusal to belong anywhere he was told to.
> For a time, they understood each other.
> Fink taught Peter how to survive without magic—how to track, steal, strike first. How to distrust anything that called itself “kind.” In return, Peter gave Fink something he’d never had:
> A place. A name that wasn’t spat.
> But Peter changed. Or maybe he just revealed what he always was.
> He wanted wonder without consequence.
> Adventure without cost.
> Friends who stayed small so he could stay big.
> Fink didn’t fit that story.
> Where Fink hardened, Peter refused to. Where Fink remembered every slight, every wound, Peter forgot. Worse—he chose to forget.
> And so the stories split.
> 
> You’ve heard of her.
> Bright. Loyal. Jealous, maybe—but harmless. A glittering companion to a boy who never grows up.
> That wasn’t how it happened.
> When Fink left—no, when he was pushed out—Peter didn’t chase him. Didn’t argue. Didn’t even seem to notice for long.
> Because it was easier to replace him.
> A softer fairy came later. Smaller. Brighter. Easier to love.
> And the name?
> “Tinker.”
> That was never hers.
> 
> Now Fink walks the wider world, wings still carrying him but never defining him. He binds them tight under leather when he can. Lets people mistake him for something else. Anything else.
> He hates being called a fairy.
> Not because it’s untrue—but because of everything the word carries:
> fragility, decoration, irrelevance.
> Fink survives through steel, instinct, and spite.
> 
> As a rogue, he is precise, quiet, and vindictive. He knows where to cut—physically and emotionally.
> As a barbarian, his fury is old and buried, erupting when the world tries to make him small again.
> 
> Either way, his fighting style is deeply unfair—ambushes, dirty tricks, improvised weapons. He doesn’t duel. He ends things.
